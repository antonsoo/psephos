# Attendance experiment

Question: does a sufficient total supply the required number in every letter
group? The ancient text describes letter groups approximately equal in their
registered numbers, but does not specify how an uneven daily turnout was handled.
The new room exposes this missing assumption rather than silently extending the
full-row model to it.

Use the Psephos mist #edf4f3, chalk #fbfdfb, patina #153d39, green #26766c,
plum #98394a and line #b6ccc4. Literata carries the question and Greek;
Source Sans 3 carries the counts and operations. The central object is five
visible stacks of possible attendees, with present and absent slots and a quota
line. The total and the shortest group are separate measurements.

```
Enough in total. One group empty.
[population/group] [quota/group]
[ A ] [ B ] [ C ] [ D ] [ E ]    total / full rows / shortages
[+- ] [+- ] [+- ] [+- ] [+- ]    keep and compare
[exchange one attendee: from / to]
[exact chance for a uniform sample of this size]
[probability by total] [weighted count patterns, paged]
[Greek and entire witness] [editable and portable study]
```

Replacing who attends preserves the total; no person changes their letter group.
The default ten can be exchanged into two full rows, or left unable to supply
any complete row. A second reveal distinguishes equally likely individual
subsets from unequally weighted count patterns. All patterns remain available
through pagination and CSV, without dumping thousands of controls onto a phone.

This remains a small modern experiment, not a reconstruction of historical daily
attendance. Avoid a second stone machine: these slots represent the finite
potential population and attendance, while the row workshop represents tickets
already placed. Use conventional labelled controls and no decorative motion.
