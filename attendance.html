The assumptions

Five letter groups of one tribe, represented by modern tags A–E. Start with four possible people per group and a request for two per group. Keep this attendance, then exchange arrivals until every group meets the request. No person changes their group.

Slots show counts, without assigning identities to positions. Filled slots are present; dashed slots are absent. The line marks the requested count.

Try the stated random-arrival assumption

Every subset of this size from the five finite groups is assumed equally likely. This is a modern assumption, not evidence about who actually arrived in Athens.

Why count patterns are not equally likely

Each potential person is distinct. A pattern such as 2–2–2–2–2 represents many different individual subsets. An extreme pattern represents fewer. The table counts those subsets, instead of treating every pattern as equally probable.

What the total cannot tell us.

Registered groups are not daily arrivals

Chapter 63.4 describes ten approximately equal letter groups within each tribe. Chapter 64.3 describes a cube for five tickets. The ancient text does not specify how officials handled a day with too few people in one group.

A modern finite population

Five represented groups, each with four, six or eight distinct possible people. The request is the same in every group. These sizes, attendance counts and equal full-row requirement are teaching assumptions. The inserter, total jury quota and later courtroom allocation are outside this model. Historical service concerned eligible male citizens with age and status restrictions (63.3), not all Athenians.

A stated sampling assumption

The exact probabilities assume an unbiased subset of a fixed size, sampled without replacement from this small population. Real jurors might travel together or choose when to attend. The random model does not establish a historical turnout rate.

A question in scholarship

David C. Mirhady and Carl Schwarz, “Dikastic Participation” (2011), pp. 753–757, investigate turnout under an explicitly random-arrival model and discuss the unanswered question of uneven groups. This bench uses a smaller, exact modern count. The article remains an online reference; the retained ancient-text pages work offline.
